# The 2025 craft beer crossroads: what the Brewers Association midyear report actually means for your brewery

The [Brewers Association just dropped its 2025 midyear report](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/2025-midyear-report/) and the headline numbers look grim on first read. Craft beer volume down 5%. Brewery count down 1%. Microbreweries down 3%. The narrative writes itself: *craft beer is in trouble.*

But the numbers tell a more interesting story than the headline. Read carefully, the 2025 data is actually a roadmap. Here's what it says, in plain English, and what it means if you're running a brewery right now.

## The headline numbers

- **9,269 operating craft breweries as of June 2025**, down 1% from 9,352 in June 2024
- **Craft beer volume down 5% year-over-year** in the first half of 2025
- **Microbreweries: -3%** brewery count
- **Taprooms: -1%** brewery count
- **Brewpubs and regional breweries: flat** (no change YoY)
- **Craft beer reach: 9.8% of legal-drinking-age adults** consumed craft beer in the past 30 days, up from 6.6% in 2013

Source: [Brewers Association 2025 Midyear Report](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/2025-midyear-report/)

Pause on that last number. **Craft beer reach is at 9.8%, up from 6.6% a decade ago.** More Americans are drinking craft beer than ever. The industry isn't dying — it's consolidating. Those are very different stories.

## The real story: who's winning, who's losing

The most important line in the report is buried in the channel data: **on-site models (taprooms and brewpubs) outperformed distribution models (microbreweries and regionals) by 1–2 percentage points in H1 2025.**

Read that twice. Taprooms are *winning* right now relative to the rest of the industry. Brewpubs are flat — which in a down year is effectively winning. **The breweries getting hurt are the ones trying to compete on grocery-store shelves** against big beer, imports, and private-label.

The math is simple: distribution is a scale game, and scale isn't craft's advantage. The breweries who built their business around **their own taproom and their own regulars** are outperforming the breweries who bet on distribution. That was true before 2025. It's *more* true now.

## What the "49/47" number really says

Buried in the same report: **49% of surveyed craft breweries reported production growth in H1 2025, while 47% reported a decline.** Small breweries (under 1,000 barrels) split 50% growth / 43% decline.

This is not a dying industry. It's a polarized one. **Almost exactly half of craft breweries are growing right now.** The question isn't "is craft beer in trouble?" It's "which half are you on, and why?"

The growing half almost uniformly has three things in common: a strong taproom, a strong local identity, and a strong relationship with their regulars. The declining half is usually chasing scale, chasing distribution, and chasing styles that were hot three years ago.

## What this means for your brewery

If you're running a brewery in 2026, here's the playbook the numbers support:

**1. Bet on your taproom.** The on-site channel is outperforming every other channel. Your taproom is your highest-margin sales floor and your strongest brand-building asset. If your taproom is an afterthought to your distribution business, flip that priority.

**2. Your regulars are worth more than new drinkers.** Acquisition costs in craft beer have gone through the roof as inflation, tariffs, and competition compound. [Industry data suggests breweries spend roughly $300 on retention for every $3 they spend on paid acquisition](https://blog.anyroad.com/post/beer-loyalty-program), because retention is the only thing that pencils out. If you don't have a system for knowing who your regulars are and what they drink, you're flying blind.

**3. Stop chasing trends.** The breweries hit hardest in 2024–2025 were the ones who went all-in on hazy IPAs in 2020, pastry stouts in 2021, hard seltzer in 2022, NA in 2023. If your business model depends on correctly guessing the next style, you're playing roulette. The breweries that are flat-to-up are the ones with a lineup that fits their identity, not a lineup that chases Instagram.

**4. Fix the data black hole.** Most small breweries know almost nothing about their own customers. Who came in last week? Who hasn't been back in 60 days? Who's about to churn? Who are the 20 regulars that drive 80% of your taproom revenue? If you can't answer those questions, you can't run the playbook above. This is the single biggest gap in small-brewery operations in 2026.

**5. Watch the competition, not the headlines.** The macro trend is "craft beer down 5%." The micro trend for the best-run taprooms is "up 10–20% year over year." Those taprooms exist in your city. Find them. Visit them. Steal their ideas.

## The crossroads moment

The industry is not shrinking randomly. It's shrinking in exactly the places where craft beer shouldn't have been playing in the first place — the shelf, the pack, the scale game. It's *holding or growing* in exactly the place craft beer was always best: the taproom, the regular, the community, the small-batch release that sells out by Sunday.

The breweries who hear "craft is down 5%" and panic are going to panic-cut marketing, panic-cut staff, and panic-chase distribution. The breweries who read the same report and double down on their taproom, their regulars, and their local community will be the ones still pouring in 2030.

Which half are you on?
